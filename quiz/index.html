1. SI / Metric System

Mass

  • 1 kg = 1,000 g
  • 1 g = 1,000 mg
  • 1 mg = 1,000 mcg (µg)
  • 1 kg = 1,000,000 mg
  • 1 g = 1,000,000 mcg

Volume

  • 1 L = 1,000 mL
  • 1 mL = 1,000 µL
  • 1 L = 1,000,000 µL

Length

  • 1 m = 100 cm = 1,000 mm
  • 1 cm = 10 mm
  • 1 km = 1,000 m

Common prefixes: kilo = 10³, hecto = 10², deka = 10¹, base = 10⁰, deci = 10⁻¹, centi = 10⁻², milli = 10⁻³, micro = 10⁻⁶.

2. Apothecaries System — Weight
  • 1 pound = 12 ounces
  • 1 ounce = 8 drams
  • 1 dram = 3 scruples
  • 1 scruple = 20 grains
  • 1 dram = 60 grains
  • 1 ounce = 480 grains
  • 1 pound = 5,760 grains

  • 1 apothecaries pound ≈ 373.2 g
3. Apothecaries System — Fluid Measure
  • 60 minims = 1 fluid dram
  • 8 fluid drams = 1 fluid ounce
  • 16 fluid ounces = 1 pint
  • 2 pints = 1 quart
  • 4 quarts = 1 gallon
  • 1 fluid ounce = 480 minims
  • 1 pint = 7,680 minims

Approximate metric equivalents:

  • 1 minim ≈ 0.0616 mL
  • 1 fluid dram ≈ 3.70 mL
  • 1 fluid ounce ≈ 29.57 mL
  • 1 pint ≈ 473 mL
  • 1 quart ≈ 946 mL
  • 1 gallon ≈ 3.785 L
4. Avoirdupois System
  • 1 pound = 16 ounces
  • 1 ounce = 16 drams
  • 1 pound = 256 drams
  • 1 stone = 14 pounds
  • 1 hundredweight = 100 pounds
  • 1 short ton = 20 hundredweight = 2,000 pounds

Approximate metric equivalents:

  • 1 ounce ≈ 28.35 g
  • 1 pound ≈ 453.6 g
  • 1 stone ≈ 6.35 kg
  • 1 hundredweight ≈ 45.36 kg
  • 1 short ton ≈ 907.2 kg
5. Quick Comparison
  • Apothecaries weight: 12 oz = 1 lb; 8 drams = 1 oz; 3 scruples = 1 dram; 20 grains = 1 scruple.
  • Avoirdupois weight: 16 oz = 1 lb; 16 drams = 1 oz.
  • SI/Metric: decimal-based, so prefixes represent powers of 10.

Do not confuse: 1 apothecaries oz ≈ 31.1 g, while 1 avoirdupois oz ≈ 28.35 g. 1 apothecaries lb ≈ 373.2 g, while 1 avoirdupois lb ≈ 453.6 g.

Introduction to Botany — Quick Notes

Short reviewer-style notes based only on the supplied lecture PDF. Use the quiz for detailed practice.

1. Botany & Branches
  • Botany — scientific study of plants.
  • Botanikos — botanical.
  • Botane — plant or herb.
  • Boskein — to feed.
  • Botanique — French for botanical.

Branches:

  • Plant Anatomy — internal structure.
  • Plant Physiology — plant function.
    • J. B. van Helmont; plants do not have the same nutritional needs as animals.
  • Plant Taxonomy — describes, names, classifies plants; oldest branch.
  • Plant Systematics — broader than taxonomy; groups organisms.
  • Plant Geology — plants of former ages; fossil remains.
  • Plant Geography — plant distribution.
  • Plant Ecology — plants and their environment/interactions.
  • Plant Morphology — form and structure.
  • Genetics — heredity; Gregor Mendel used pea plants.
  • Genomics — genes and their function.
  • Cell Biology — cell structure and function.
  • Economic Botany & Ethnobotany — practical uses of plants/products: food, fiber, medicines, etc.
2. Early Botanists & Contributions
  • Leon Ma. Guerrero
    • Father of Botany in the Philippines
    • National Scientist
    • Father of Pharmacy in the Philippines
  • Theophrastus
    • Father of Botany
  • Philippus Aureolus Bombastus von Hohenheim
    • Pioneered minerals/chemicals in medicine
    • Examples: mercury, lead, arsenic, antimony
    • Helped introduce chemistry into medicine
    • Lecture point: poison depends on the dose
  • Shen Nung
    • Associated with Pen T'sao
    • Traditional Chinese medical literature on medicinal substances
    • Lecture describes testing herbs on himself
  • Robert Hooke
    • Observed cork
    • Saw tiny box-like structures
    • Called them cells
  • Robert Brown
    • Discovered the plant cell's nucleus
    • Lecture also mentions erratic pollen-grain movement in water
  • Rudolf Virchow
    • “Omnis cellula e cellula”
    • Cells are living things
    • Cellular pathology: disease involves changes in normal cells
  • 7 Linnaean classifications — listed as a historical/taxonomic heading in the lecture.
3. Origin of Life
  • Panspermia / Extraterrestrial Origin
    • Given by Richer in 1865
    • Also called cosmozoic theory
    • Panspermia = “seeds everywhere”
    • Life's “seeds” are proposed to exist throughout the Universe
    • They may travel through space from one location to another
  • Spontaneous Generation
    • Associated with Aristotle
    • Living beings could arise from inert/non-living matter
    • No pre-existing body involved in the proposed creation
    • Lecture describes its adoption as a doctrine that merged with creationist concepts supported by the Church
4. Evolution & Natural Selection
  • Scientists: Charles Darwin + Alfred Russel Wallace
  • Theory: Evolution by Natural Selection
  • Natural selection:
    • Organisms produce more offspring than can survive.
    • Better-equipped organisms are more likely to survive, mature, and reproduce.
    • Less-fit organisms may not reach reproductive age.
    • They may also produce fewer offspring.
5. Plant Adaptation
  • Behavioral — responses that support survival/reproduction.
  • Physiological — body processes/metabolism important for survival/reproduction.
  • Structural — physical traits that support survival/reproduction.
6. Behavioral Adaptations
  • Nature:
    • Usually not passed from generation to generation in the lecture's description
    • Acquired during an individual's lifetime
    • Allow quick responses to environmental changes
  • Phototropism / Heliotropism — response for getting sunlight
    • Plants grow/lean toward the sun
    • Vines climb trees for sunlight
    • Shoots grow toward light to maximize photosynthetic yield
  • Thigmotropism — response to touch
    • May grow toward the touch
    • May respond away from the touch
  • Gravitropism / Geotropism — response to gravity
    • Stems grow away from gravity
    • Roots grow in the opposite direction
  • Water & nutrients
    • Roots penetrate deep into soil
    • Desert flowers may remain dormant until rain
  • Why they occur:
    • Respond to changing light, water, temperature, and predation
    • Use available resources
    • Protect against threats
    • Support reproduction and fitness
7. Physiological Adaptations & Homeostasis
  • Physiological adaptation — continuous cellular, biochemical, and metabolic changes.
  • Purpose:
    • Keep the organism balanced
    • Improve adaptation to environmental change
    • Respond to environmental stimulation
    • Help preserve homeostasis
  • Inheritance: genetically determined and transmissible to the next generation.
  • Homeostasis — self-regulation that maintains internal stability while conditions change.
  • Homeostasis goals:
    • Maintain water/nutrient uptake from soil to leaves
    • Control stomatal opening
    • Minimize water loss
    • Maximize CO₂ absorption
  • Significance:
    • Supports survival in ecological niches
    • Supports healthy growth and development
    • Helps manage temperature, pressure, ionic balance, and metabolic rates
    • Conserves and maximizes resources
8. Structural Adaptations
  • Structural adaptation — physical feature evolved to help survival in a specific environment.
  • Can occur in: leaves, stems, roots, reproductive structures.
  • Helps with: water scarcity, extreme temperatures, poor soil, predation.
  • Usually: inheritable and transgenerational.
  • Reproduction:
    • Bright petals attract pollinators
    • Pollinators listed: birds, bees, insects
  • Seed dispersal:
    • Sweet fruits attract animals
    • Animals spread seeds
  • Defense: spines and/or hairs.
  • Food & water conservation:
    • Desert plants may have stout stems
    • Desert plants may have fewer leaves
    • Used as a heat-coping strategy
  • Significance:
    • Survival and reproduction
    • Allows plants to live in diverse habitats
    • Examples: deserts, wetlands, forests, mountains
    • Maximizes resources
    • Defends against herbivores
    • Supports continuation of the species
